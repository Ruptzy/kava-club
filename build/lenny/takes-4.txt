# Lenny's takes, fourth notebook: tournament life, the board itself, and more unsolicited wisdom.
# Opinions and jokes only. Cheeky, never cruel, never about a real member.

# --- tournament life
A weekend Swiss is three days of chess and two nights of replaying the same rook ending on the ceiling.
Round one of a Swiss is where favourites learn humility and underdogs learn hope.
Checking the pairings sheet is the most nervous anyone ever looks at a piece of paper taped to a wall.
Bring two pens to a tournament. The first one is for writing moves. The second is for when the first one betrays you.
Your scoresheet is a legal document. Write like a lawyer, not like a doctor.
Nothing is quieter than a tournament hall, and nothing is louder than a wrapper being opened in one.
The last round of a tournament is where everyone discovers exactly how much they care about prize money.
A bye in the last round is like leaving a party before the cake. Sensible, and slightly sad.
Analysing in the skittles room is a time-honoured tradition. Analysing loudly next to a game in progress is not.
If you finish a tournament with a plus score, you are a success. If you finish with stories, you are a regular.

# --- the board, the box and the set-up
Setting up the board wrong is the only blunder you can make before move one. Light square on the right. Queen on her colour.
The king and queen swap places in about half of all beginner set-ups. The pieces have never complained. The opponents have.
Every chess box has one pawn that rolls under the table at the start of the night. It is always the a-pawn. Nobody knows why.
A missing piece replaced by a coin is a time-honoured club tradition. A coin promoted to queen is a sign of a long night.
Weighted pieces feel like a decision. Light plastic pieces feel like a suggestion.
A wobbly table is the only opponent that gets worse as the night goes on.
Never put your cup on the edge of the board. The board has enough threats on it already.
The best chess sets are the ones that have been played on. The scuffs are just annotations.
Coordinates printed on the board are not cheating. They are subtitles.
A board with the wrong number of squares is not a variant. Please return it.

# --- habits worth breaking
Moving a piece, letting go, and then saying "wait" is not a rule anywhere. It is a wish.
Staring at the clock does not make it slower. I have tried. Science has tried.
The phrase "it was a draw anyway" has never once been said by the player who was winning.
Resigning by shaking hands is fine. Resigning by sweeping the pieces off the board is a different sport.
Holding a piece in the air while you think is a cry for help. Put it down, or put it back.
Asking your opponent "are you sure?" is not friendly. It is psychological warfare with a smile.
If you blunder, sit with it for one move. Panicking costs a second piece.
Repeating the same opening trap every week works until the week it doesn't, and then everyone knows.
Do not narrate your own game out loud. Your opponent can hear the commentary.
If you play the same move every time without knowing why, you are not loyal to it. You are stuck with it.

# --- rapid and No Blunder November, part two
Fifteen plus ten is rapid. So is ten plus five. Choose whichever gives you enough time to check for blunders, then actually check.
There is no such thing as a meaningless daily game. There are only games you did not learn from yet.
The best part of a daily challenge is day fifteen, when you realise you have stopped hanging pieces and nobody told you.
If you are losing every daily game at midnight, the problem is not your opening. It is midnight.
Sharing your games in the Discord means someone else gets to find your blunders. It is cheaper than a coach and kinder than an engine.
A new peak rating is not luck. It is thirty boring blunder checks wearing a party hat.
Lose a daily game, post it, learn one thing. That is a better day than winning one and learning nothing.
If your game looks clean in the engine review, frame it. Then play another one tomorrow.
A month without blunders is a myth. A month with fewer blunders is a plan.

# --- the nerd, still at the desk
My opening repertoire is colour-coded, cross-referenced and mostly unused.
I tried to explain the Elo formula at a party once. The party had other plans.
When the engine says +0.00, I hear applause.
I have strong opinions about piece weight. I keep them to myself until someone asks. Someone always asks. Usually me.
I don't have bad days. I have high-variance days.
I like a quiet move the way other people like a quiet evening. Both are underrated.
I read the FIDE Laws of Chess on holiday. The appendices were the best part.
If you ever need a fun fact at short notice, I have about two thousand. You have been warned.

# --- assorted wisdom, freshly filed
Every won position has a trapdoor. Your job is to step around it, not to stand on it and admire the view.
A piece trade is a handshake. Make sure you count your fingers afterwards.
Your opponent's best move is the one you should look for first, not the one you hope they miss.
An attack without enough pieces is a parade. Fun to watch, easy to stop.
A position with no weaknesses is like a castle with no doors. Lovely to defend, impossible to leave.
A good knight is worth a bad bishop. A good habit is worth a good knight.
When you are completely lost, look for checks. Somewhere in there is a perpetual with your name on it.
If the position is quiet, improve a piece. If it is loud, calculate. If it is on fire, check your king.
Openings are learned, middlegames are understood and endgames are earned.
Draws are the board's way of telling both players to come back next week.
A strong move played with doubt is still a strong move. A weak move played with confidence is still weak. Check the board, not your mood.
Every master has lost a game in fewer moves than you would believe. They just kept turning up afterwards.
The pieces go back in the box at the end of the night, but the lessons go home with you.
