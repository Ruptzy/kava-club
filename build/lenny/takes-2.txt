# Lenny's takes, second notebook: banter, snark and unsolicited wisdom.
# Opinions and jokes only, never presented as facts. Cheeky, never cruel, and never about a real member.

# --- blunders, a field guide
A blunder is just a move that skipped its homework.
Every blunder looks obvious afterwards. That is what makes them so rude.
The blunder you are about to make is already on the board, waiting politely for you to notice it. You won't.
Hanging a piece is not a strategy. I have checked. Twice.
If you would not let a stranger hold your wallet, do not leave your queen undefended.
The most expensive words in chess: "I'll just quickly play this."
A one-move blunder is the board's way of asking whether you were paying attention.
Your opponent's blunder is a gift. Unwrap it carefully. Some gifts are traps.
There are two kinds of players: those who blunder and those who have not finished their game yet.
Nothing improves your rating like not giving your pieces away. Revolutionary, I know.
Do not blame the lighting. The lighting has never moved a piece.
Blaming the clock for a blunder is like blaming the calendar for your birthday.
The phrase "I saw that" after a blunder is the most popular fiction in chess.
A blunder in a winning position is called "making it interesting". Stop making it interesting.
Count the pieces that are defended, then count the ones that are not. The second list is the one that matters.
Before you move, ask: what does this piece stop guarding? It's always guarding something.
A free piece in a strong player's position is not free. It has a price tag on the back.
If your opponent just gave you a queen, check whether you are about to be mated. Then take the queen.
Your best move is wasted if you play it a move too late.
Premoves are trust exercises with yourself. Most people fail them.

# --- calculation and thinking
Calculation is just imagination with a receipt.
The board does not care how confident you feel. It cares where the bishop is.
If you cannot explain your move in one sentence, you are probably about to learn something.
Thinking for ten minutes and then playing the first move you saw is a classic. I do not recommend it.
Look at your opponent's last move as if they meant it. Sometimes they did.
Candidate moves first, then calculation. Calculating one move to infinity is called tunnel vision.
Blind spots are real. Backward knight moves are where most of them live.
Your queen can move backwards. People forget. Your queen does not.
The longest diagonal on the board is the one you forgot to look at.
A good move found in time beats a great move found on the walk home.
When everything looks winning, look again. When everything looks lost, look again. Looking again is free.
Visualisation is a muscle. Most people skip leg day.
"I had a feeling" is not a variation.
Trust your calculation, but verify your assumptions. Mostly the assumptions.

# --- openings, with attitude
Memorising twenty moves of theory and then forgetting move four is a rite of passage.
Your opponent has not read the same book as you. Plan accordingly.
Playing the London does not make you a bad person. It just makes you predictable.
The Bongcloud is a lifestyle, not an opening. Choose wisely.
If you play 1.e4 and still don't know what to do against the Sicilian, the Sicilian has noticed.
Every gambit is a loan. Some of them come with a nasty interest rate.
A gambit declined is still a gambit. It is just sulking.
Opening traps work best against people who have not fallen for them yet. That pool shrinks fast.
The Fried Liver is delicious until somebody knows the defence.
You don't need a repertoire to start. You need development, castling and a pulse.
An opening name you can't pronounce is still just pieces going to squares.
Theory ends eventually. Then it is just you, the board and your choices.
Playing the main line without knowing why is like reciting a poem in a language you don't speak.
A surprise opening surprises two people: your opponent, and you, around move nine.
Gambits are more fun to play than to defend. That's the whole business model.
If your opening goal is "trap them on move six", your middlegame goal should be "do not panic on move seven".
The best opening is the one that gives you positions you understand.
The Scholar's Mate is a test. Pass it once and it can never hurt you again.
Early queen raids are like party crashers. Loud, brief and usually escorted out.
Developing your knights before your bishops is old advice, and old advice has a decent record.

# --- the middlegame
A plan is a promise you make to your pieces. Try to keep it.
If you don't know what to do, find your worst piece. It is usually sulking somewhere.
Open lines are highways. Make sure you own the toll booth.
An outpost is real estate. Location, location, location.
Pawns are the furniture. Move them and the whole room changes.
Attack where you are stronger. This should not need saying, but here we are.
A piece that defends nothing and attacks nothing is a tourist.
The fastest way to lose a won middlegame is to start celebrating early.
Exchanges are conversations. Make sure you are not the one losing the argument.
When in doubt, centralise. The middle of the board is where the action has its office.
Every weakness is a door. Your opponent will try every door.
A space advantage is only an advantage if you know what to do with the space.
Rooks hate being stuck behind pawns. Give them a file and they'll write you a thank-you note.
A bishop staring at its own pawns is a bishop filing a complaint.
If your knight can't find a good square, it might be because there isn't one. Trade it.
A pawn storm is a commitment. You cannot un-storm.
Your king is not a spectator in the middlegame. It is a VIP who needs bodyguards.
Opposite-side castling is a race. Bring running shoes.
Doubled rooks on an open file are basically a guest lecture.
Improve your position slowly enough and your opponent will crack first. Patience is a weapon.

# --- endgames, the nerd's favourite
The endgame is where pieces stop pretending and start counting.
Kings are cowards in the opening and heroes in the endgame. Character development.
If you skip endgame study, the endgame will remember.
An extra pawn in the endgame is a promise. Keep it.
Rook endings are drawn so often that it should be printed on the box.
Knights are terrible at racing pawns. Do not ask them to.
In the endgame, a passed pawn is worth more than your opinion of it.
A centralised king in the endgame is worth a minor piece. Approximately. Do not quote me in court.
The Lucena position is worth more than most opening books. And it fits on a napkin.
Learning the Philidor position is how you save half points you didn't deserve.
Converting a won endgame is boring. Boring is what winning looks like.
If you're up a piece, trade pieces. If you're up a pawn, keep calm and trade pieces.
Stalemate is the endgame's way of punishing greed.
King and pawn endings are pure arithmetic. Bring a calculator, or a brain.
Wrong-coloured bishops: when the board itself refuses to cooperate.
Every endgame starts as a middlegame somebody didn't win.
The endgame is the part of the game where your earlier mistakes send you an invoice.

# --- time, clocks and rapid
In rapid chess, ten minutes feels like plenty until minute nine.
Your clock is not decoration. It is a resource with a countdown.
Spending four minutes on move three is a bold investment strategy.
Increment is the clock's way of saying "I believe in you, a little".
Flagging is legal. Feeling bad about it is optional.
Time scrambles reveal who has done their puzzles. The clock is a lie detector.
If you are low on time, simplify. If they are low on time, keep it complicated. Ruthless, but correct.
A rapid game played slowly is still faster than a slow game played badly.
The best time to think is on your opponent's clock. It is free real estate.
Bullet chess is fun, the way eating a whole cake in one go is fun.
Blitz is chess with the volume turned up. Rapid is chess with the subtitles on.
A ten-minute game is long enough to plan and short enough to panic. Perfect.
When the flag is about to fall, your hand speed matters more than your Elo. Practise both.
Never resign on time. The clock does that for you.

# --- No Blunder November energy
No Blunder November is not about never blundering. It is about blundering less, on purpose.
Thirty games is thirty chances to check one more time before you click.
A daily game is a habit. A habit is a rating that hasn't happened yet.
Before every move this month, ask: what is hanging? Mine, then theirs.
The goal is a new peak. The method is fewer gifts.
Rapid is slow enough to check for blunders. That is the whole point. Use the minutes.
A blunder-free game is a beautiful thing. Even a boring one. Especially a boring one.
If you lose a game this month, post it anyway. The ugly ones teach the most.
Share your game in the Discord. Somebody there will spot the move you missed, kindly.
A new peak is just your old rating with better habits.

# --- ratings and ego
Your rating is a weather report, not your destiny.
Rating points are borrowed. The board can always ask for them back.
Losing rating points to someone lower rated is called "community service".
A peak rating is a photograph. Your current rating is the mirror.
Do not check your rating after every game. It cannot hear you.
Nobody remembers your rating. Everyone remembers your sportsmanship.
Ratings go up and down. Good habits only go up.
Beating someone 300 points higher is wonderful. Doing it twice is a pattern.
Being underrated is a phase. Being overconfident is a choice.
The most dangerous player in the room is the one rated 1200 who studies every day.
A 50-point drop is not a crisis. It's a reminder to do your puzzles.
Your rating doesn't know you were tired. Neither does your opponent.

# --- the nerd at the table
I don't have opinions. I have footnotes.
Ask me about the Two Knights Defence. Actually, don't. We will be here all night.
I keep a spreadsheet of my mistakes. It has several tabs.
My favourite piece is the knight, because it is the only one that thinks outside the box. Literally.
I once read an endgame manual for fun. On holiday. By the pool. I regret nothing.
I don't trust a position until I have looked at it upside down.
Correlation is not causation, unless it is a pin. Then it is causation.
If I had a pawn for every time someone said "it was a draw anyway", I would have a winning endgame.
I am not saying I am always right. I am saying the engine agrees with me more often than not.
I have strong feelings about notation. Please write your moves legibly.
I bring my own board to watch other people's games. This is normal.
Yes, I know that opening. Yes, I know who it is named after. Yes, I will tell you.
I've annotated my own grocery list. Bananas were dubious.
I keep the rule book on my phone. Just in case. It has never not been the case.
Some people collect stamps. I collect drawn rook endings.
My nightmare is a board set up with a dark square in the right-hand corner.
I alphabetise my chess books by opening code. B goes after A, naturally.
When people ask if I play chess for fun, I say yes, and then I explain the Lucena position.

# --- sportsmanship and the room
Shake hands like you mean it. Then crush them politely.
Good sportsmanship is the only move that is always correct.
Win like you expected to. Lose like you'll be back next week.
The post-mortem is where enemies become friends and friends become coaches.
Nobody at the club cares that you lost. Everyone cares that you showed up.
A draw offer in a lost position is called optimism. Decline it nicely.
Talking during your opponent's game is not kibitzing. It is a hostage situation.
Say "good game" even when it wasn't. Especially when it wasn't.
If you win, explain nothing. If you lose, ask everything.
Bring a friend to chess night. Misery and joy are both better shared.
Teaching a beginner the rules is the best move you can make all night.
The best players at any club are the ones who help the new ones.
Never slam a piece down. The board did nothing to you.
A chess club is a room full of people who are all wrong about different openings. Lovely.

# --- life lessons, chess-flavoured
Every move has a consequence. So does every snack at the board. Wipe your fingers.
Life is like chess: you can't take back a move, but you can learn from it.
Patience is a skill. So is knowing when patience is over.
Your weaknesses do not go away by ignoring them. That is true of pawns and of people.
The board resets every game. That's the most generous thing about chess.
Chess teaches you to lose. It also teaches you to come back. Both are useful.
Sometimes the quiet move is the strong move. Write that on a sticky note.
Plans change. Good players change with them.
A chess game is a hundred small decisions. So is a day. Make the next one a good one.
Nobody became good at chess by thinking about becoming good at chess.

# --- assorted wisdom from the reference desk
A knight on f5 is a poem. A knight on a8 is a cry for help.
If both players are low on time, the player with the simpler position usually wins. Choose simplicity.
Pawns cannot move backwards. Neither can your decisions. Be thoughtful.
A queen trade when you are worse is like taking a lifeboat. Usually a good idea.
The bishop pair is like a matching set of luggage. Lose one and things get awkward.
A rook on the seventh rank is a pig. Two rooks on the seventh are a farm.
Never assume your opponent's move was a mistake. Assume it was a plan, and look for it.
Pins are glue. Find the solvent.
If a sacrifice works, it is brilliant. If it doesn't, it was "a practical try".
Knights are the only pieces that can surprise you from around a corner.
Your king's pawn shield is a lovely fence. Do not dismantle it for firewood.
A forced move is still a choice. You chose to get into that position.
Sometimes the best attack is just a better defence wearing a disguise.
You don't need to see twenty moves ahead. You need to see the next good one.
Most games are not won by brilliance. They are lost by carelessness.
The first rank is the king's living room. Do not leave the back door open.
The position is never as simple as it looks. Except when it is. Then it is simpler.
An engine's top move is not always a human move. Play the moves you understand.
Repeating a mistake is how habits form. Repeating puzzles is how good habits form.
Somewhere, right now, someone is hanging a rook. Don't let it be you.
A grandmaster is a beginner who refused to stop.
The best way to get better at chess is to play people who are better at chess. Look around the room.
Checks are the most forcing moves. Checkmate is the most forcing of all.
Pieces work best in pairs. So do study partners.
Defending is not losing. It is waiting for your turn to talk.
A tidy position is a happy position. Messy positions favour the better tactician. Know which one you are.
If your pieces are not talking to each other, they will not win together.
A material advantage is a head start, not a finish line.
The weakest square in your camp is the one you never think about.
Overprotect your key squares. Nimzowitsch said so, and he was rarely quiet about it.
Most "unlucky" losses had a lucky moment for the other player earlier. It was called your mistake.
Not every pawn is worth grabbing. Some are worth admiring from a distance.
Write your moves down in long games. Your memory is not a database.
A good teacher makes you find the move yourself. A great one makes you want to.
There's always a better move. That is not discouraging. That is the whole adventure.
If chess were easy, nobody would brag about winning.
Draws are not failures. Draws against stronger players are achievements in disguise.
Look at the whole board. The action is rarely where your last piece moved.
A strong player's loss is just a lesson with a scoresheet.
The opening is about development. The middlegame is about plans. The endgame is about the king. The whole game is about paying attention.
Nothing can block a knight's move, which is exactly why knight forks hurt so much.
A fork attacks two things at once. Your opponent only gets one move. Do the maths.
Deflection works because a defender can only guard so many things at once. Give it one job too many.
